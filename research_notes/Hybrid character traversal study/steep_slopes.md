# Steep slopes: walking, scrambling and descending (sourced notes)

Researched 25 Nov 2026. Every item under "Cited findings" comes from a page or PDF fetched in that session.
Where a paper was read as a PDF or through the Europe PMC abstract service, the link says so.
Angles are in degrees from horizontal. Gradient = rise / run = tan(angle).

## Summary (5-8 lines)

- People still walk straight up slopes of 30 to 39 degrees, but slowly: lab treadmill walking at 30 degrees was done at 0.3 to 0.8 m/s along the belt, and the world's best racers manage about 0.6 m/s of vertical gain on courses averaging about 27 to 31 degrees.
- Above about 16 degrees walking is cheaper than running; the cheapest slopes for gaining height are 20 to 35 degrees. Steep "running" has no flight phase.
- Tobler's hiking function gives 1.41 / 0.67 / 0.27 km/h uphill and 2.00 / 0.95 / 0.38 km/h downhill at 20 / 30 / 40 degrees. Real GPS data says downhill is faster than uphill on the same slope.
- No fetched source gives a slope angle at which hands come into use. Grading systems define it by terrain: hands "occasionally" at YDS class 2, "for balance" at SAC T3, "needed to move forward" at SAC T4.
- Rubber on dry concrete (mu 0.6 to 0.85) holds a standing person to about 31 to 40 degrees by tan(theta) = mu; wet surfaces and grass are much lower; climbing rubber on granite reaches mu about 1.0 to 1.2.
- Descent: straight down with bent knees and weight over the feet on firm ground; traverse on grass; short shuffling heel-dug steps on scree; plunge step or glissade on snow; turn and face in on scramble steps.
- Hands-and-feet crawling is comfortable at about 3 km/h and most adults use a lateral footfall sequence with no fixed limb pairing.

## Cited findings

### 1. Speed versus slope
- Tobler's hiking function: W = 6 e^(-3.5 |dh/dx + 0.05|), W in km/h, dh/dx = tan(theta). Maximum 6 km/h at about -2.86 degrees. Flat speed 5 km/h. Off-path: multiply by 3/5. Fitted to data of Eduard Imhof. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tobler%27s_hiking_function
- Tobler table values: +20 deg 1.41 km/h, +30 deg 0.67, +40 deg 0.27, +50 deg 0.08; -20 deg 2.00, -30 deg 0.95, -40 deg 0.38, -50 deg 0.11, -60 deg 0.02 km/h. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tobler%27s_hiking_function
- Naismith's rule (1892): "one hour for every 3 miles (5 km) forward, plus an additional hour for every 2,000 feet (600 m) of ascent." https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naismith%27s_rule
- Langmuir corrections: base 5 km/h; on gentle descent (5 to 12 degrees) subtract 10 minutes per 300 m of descent; on steep descent (over 12 degrees) add 10 minutes per 300 m of descent. Aitken: 5 km/h on paths, 4 km/h on other surfaces. Scarf: 1 unit of climb equals 7.92 units of distance. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naismith%27s_rule
- BMC's modern Naismith: "4 or 5 kms per hour, with the addition of 1 minute for every 10 metres of height gained". https://services.thebmc.co.uk/the-art-of-descent
- GPS study (Campbell et al. 2019, Applied Geography): 29,928 individuals, 421,247 hikes, jogs and runs near Salt Lake City; slopes greater than 30 degrees were removed, so the model covers plus or minus 30 degrees. It states Tobler's peak as about 1.67 m/s at -2.86 degrees. https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10097294 (PDF)
- Press summary of the same study: a slow walk over a flat mile takes about 33 minutes, the same exertion "on a steep, 30 degree slope will take about 97 minutes"; a fast run takes about 6 minutes flat and 13 minutes up 30 degrees; "walking down a steep slope of 30 degrees was done at the same speed as walking up a slope of 16 degrees". https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2019/04/190403101108.htm
- Treadmill, 7 angles at a fixed vertical velocity of 0.35 m/s (Giovanelli et al. 2016): 9.4, 15.8, 20.4, 24.8, 30.0, 35.0, 39.2 degrees. Walking was cheaper than running at all angles except 9.4 (average -8.45 %). Cost minima between 20.4 and 35.0 degrees. "on inclines steeper than 15.8 degrees, athletes can reduce their energy expenditure by walking rather than running." https://europepmc.org/abstract/MED/26607247 (abstract read through Europe PMC)
- Belt speeds for those angles (author manuscript in N. Giovanelli's thesis): 2.14, 1.29, 1.00, 0.83, 0.70, 0.61, 0.51 m/s. At 9.4 degrees only 9 of 15 subjects could walk at 2.14 m/s. https://air.uniud.it/bitstream/11390/1132913/1/10990_676_PhD%20thesis%20final_Giovanelli_print.pdf (PDF)
- Treadmill at 30 degrees (Ortiz et al. 2017): belt speed started at 0.3 m/s and rose by 0.1 m/s until subjects failed. Walking was cheaper than running between 0.3 and 0.7 m/s; no difference at 0.8 m/s. https://europepmc.org/abstract/MED/28695271 (abstract read through Europe PMC)
- Treadmill gradients -0.45 to +0.45 (Minetti et al. 2002): lowest walking speed 0.69 m/s at every gradient. Highest tested running speed 0.89 +/- 0.10 m/s at +0.45, 1.46 at +0.30, 2.11 at +0.20, 3.13 on the level, 2.84 at -0.20, 2.52 at -0.30, 1.63 +/- 0.40 at -0.45. Optimum gradient for mountain paths about 0.20 to 0.30. Cheapest downhill walking at -0.10. https://iris.unibs.it/retrieve/ddc633e4-3c03-4e2e-e053-3705fe0a4c80/Minetti%20JAP%202002.pdf (PDF)

### 2. Steepest slopes walked or run
- Vertical kilometer: 1,000 m of gain in less than 5 km. Men's record 27:21 (Remi Bonnet, 2025, Fully); women's 32:52 (Axelle Mollaret, 2025, Fully). Previous men's record 28:53 (Philip Goetsch, 2017, Fully). https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vertical_kilometer
- Fully course: 1,920 m long, 1,000 m gain (500 m to 1,500 m), "52 % de moyenne", some slopes "a plus de 60 %". https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kilom%C3%A8tre_vertical_de_Fully
- Red Bull report of the record: 1.92 km, average gradient "more than 50%", poles recommended, 18 October 2025. https://www.redbull.com/int-en/remi-bonnet-breaks-vertical-kilometre-world-record
- "the inclines of the fastest [VK] courses are about 30 degrees". https://europepmc.org/abstract/MED/26607247
- Manitou Incline: about 0.88 miles (1.42 km) horizontal, 2,011 ft (613 m) gain, average grade 45 % (24 degrees), steepest 68 % (34 degrees), about 2,768 steps. Records 17:16 (men) and 20:07 (women). https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manitou_Incline
- Red Bull 400: 400 m up a ski jump, "maximum incline angle of 37 degrees (75%)". https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_Bull_400
- The 2012 Planica 400 m event is described by the venue as an "on-all-four" run; 146 men and 18 women took part. https://www.planica.si/en/news-archive/news/2012-09-24-400-meter-run-on-the-flying-hill
- Mount Marathon Race: about 3.1 miles (5 km), turn point at 2,974 ft (906 m). "Average speed uphill is 2 mph. Average speed downhill is 12 mph." Leaders take 33 to 40 minutes up and 10 to 15 minutes down. Record 40:37. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Marathon_Race
- Steepest street (Baldwin Street): maximum "about 1:2.86 (19 degrees or 35%)"; 34.8 % on the centre line. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baldwin_Street
- Uphill races in Minetti's Table 3: mean gradient 0.360 at 0.460 vertical m/s (2.9 km, 981 m, 2,132 s); Cervinia vertical km 0.298 at 0.393 vertical m/s. Downhill races: 0.489 to 0.947 vertical m/s on mean gradients -0.105 to -0.245. https://iris.unibs.it/retrieve/ddc633e4-3c03-4e2e-e053-3705fe0a4c80/Minetti%20JAP%202002.pdf

### 3. When hands come in, and posture on steep ground
- Yosemite Decimal System: Class 1 "Hiking or running on a trail." Class 2 "Simple scrambling, with the possibility of occasional use of the hands." Class 3 "Scrambling. A rope could be carried." Class 4 "Simple climbing, possibly with exposure... Falls could be fatal." https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yosemite_Decimal_System
- US Army version: Class 1 hiking trail; Class 2 off-trail scramble; Class 3 moderate scrambling; Class 5.0 to 5.4 "Hands are necessary to support balance"; Class 5.5 "Three points of contact are necessary." Mountain slopes "commonly range from 4 to 45 degrees". https://archive.org/download/MManuals/Fm3-97UsArmyMilitaryMountaineering2002.pdf (FM 3-97.61, PDF)
- Scrambling is "Walk up steep terrain involving the use of one's hands". UK grades 1, 2, 3, 3S. No angle is given. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scrambling
- BMC: "All scrambling grades require a degree of rock climbing as both hands and feet are employed, but a classic grade 1 scramble is essentially an exposed walking route." Grade 3 may be up to about Difficult in rock climbing standards. https://services.thebmc.co.uk/understanding-scrambling-grades
- Swiss SAC hiking scale (translated): T3 "You may need your hands for balance"; T4 "In certain places, hands are needed to move forward"; T6 has climbing sections up to grade II. No angles given. https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/SAC-Wanderskala
- Rest step (FM 3-97.61): used "for steep slopes, snowfields, and higher elevations". After each step the soldier pauses, "resting his entire bodyweight on the rear leg. The rear leg is kept straight with the knee locked so that bone, not muscle, supports the weight." Steeper slopes "may require several breaths per step". Same PDF.
- Uphill progression on hard ground (FM 3-97.61): "from walking straight up, to a herringbone step [toes pointed out], and then to a traverse on the steeper areas." When traversing, the full sole is kept on the ground "by rolling the ankle downhill on each step"; turn by stepping off with the uphill foot. Stride length is adjusted to hold tempo on changing slope. Same PDF.
- Climbing stance (FM 3-97.61): body near vertical "with the weight centered over the feet"; hands "primarily for balance"; "Leaning in towards the rock will cause the feet to push outward... resulting in a loss of friction"; heels kept low; "Whenever possible, three points of contact are maintained"; move one of five body parts at a time. Same PDF.
- Grass (FM 3-97.61): going up, step on the upper side of each tussock; "Grassy slopes are slippery after rain, new snow, and dew"; use flat-hand push holds, not finger pulls. Same PDF.
- Scree uphill (FM 3-97.61): kick the toe of the upper foot in to form a step, test it, then transfer weight. Same PDF.
- New Zealand mountain safety text: "Use tussocks to haul yourself up with in steep country". https://wilderlife.nz/in-the-mountains/above-the-bushline/
- Ankle limit: leaning forward on a slope is done by flexing the ankle; one measured person "could not bend his/her ankle by more than 24" degrees, so "good foot contact cannot be maintained with the ground when walking directly up very steep inclines"; walking on a 10 degree incline needs 60 degrees of hip flexion against 30 on the flat. Zig-zag paths form spontaneously; photographed examples on inclines of about 1:8 and 1:2. https://arxiv.org/pdf/0812.1536 (Gilks and Hague 2009)

### 4. Grip limits
- Rubber on dry concrete 0.6 to 0.85; on wet concrete 0.45 to 0.75; on dry asphalt 0.9 static and 0.5 to 0.8 kinetic; on wet asphalt 0.25 to 0.75; car tire on grass 0.35; rubber on rubber 1.16. https://www.engineeringtoolbox.com/friction-coefficients-d_778.html
- Climbing shoe rubber on granite, 5 compounds, 30 N and 60 N loads: static 0.97 to 1.21, dynamic 0.76 to 1.14 (Table 6). https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/217969/1/s12283-024-00474-4.pdf (Sports Engineering 2024, PDF)
- Required friction on ramps, as cited by Pollard et al. 2015: descending, required friction rose from 0.2 on the level to 0.45 at 20 degrees (Redfern and DiPasquale 1997); at 20 degrees it ranged 0.6 to 1.10 ascending and 0.45 to 0.60 descending (McVay and Redfern 1994). Slip probability is 1 % when available friction exceeds required friction by 0.52 and 50 % when it exceeds it by 0.16 (Hanson et al. 1999). https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4714842
- Pollard's own grated walkways at 0 to 20 degrees: mean required friction 0.17 to 0.31 uphill and 0.22 to 0.64 downhill; prior work says uphill walking lengthens stride and lowers cadence, downhill shortens stride and raises cadence. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4714842
- "Be extremely careful when climbing on wet or moss-covered rock"; "Wet grass can be extremely slippery". FM 3-97.61 PDF (URL above).

### 5. Going down
- Hard ground (FM 3-97.61): "Descending is best done by walking straight down the slope without traversing." Back straight, knees bent to absorb shock, "Body weight is kept directly over the feet and the full boot sole is placed on the ground with each step. Walking with a slight forward lean and with the feet in a normal position make the descent easier." Moderate pace. Same PDF.
- Grass (FM 3-97.61): "the traverse technique should be used... A climber can easily build up too much speed and fall if a direct descent is tried." Hop-skip step: the lower leg takes all the weight. Uphill foot points along travel, downhill foot about 45 degrees downhill. Same PDF.
- Scree (FM 3-97.61): "come straight down the slope using a short shuffling step with the knees bent, back straight, feet pointed downhill, and heels dug in." Avoid running. Same PDF.
- Snow (FM 3-97.61): soft and gentle, walk straight down. "Harder snow or steeper slopes call for the plunge step": face out, drive the heel in with the leg straight; on steeper terrain squat on the weighted leg; upper body "erect or canted slightly forward". Same PDF.
- Glissade (FM 3-97.61): "intentional, controlled, rapid descent, or slide". Squatting glissade: semi-crouched, weight over the feet. Sitting glissade: sitting, heels raised, "slower but easier to control". Never where the bottom cannot be seen. Same PDF.
- "Depending on the terrain, face inwards for steep slopes, but otherwise face out." On steep tussock, slide each foot from one tussock to the next while gripping others; "It is acceptable to slide on one's bum from tussock to tussock as a last resort." On scree, "sit down if one loses one's balance". https://wilderlife.nz/in-the-mountains/above-the-bushline/
- BMC: accidents on simple scrambles "are frequent due to reluctance to turn inwards and face the rock while negotiating the descent." https://services.thebmc.co.uk/the-art-of-descent
- Walking posture downhill (treadmill, 0 to -10 % grade): stride length falls, trunk and pelvis tilt progressively backward, knee flexion rises in weight acceptance and late stance. https://europepmc.org/abstract/MED/11809582 (Leroux et al. 2002, abstract read through Europe PMC)

### 6. Biomechanics of steep incline walking
- 30 degrees at 1.0 m/s (Whiting et al. 2020): steep uphill walking shows two vertical acceleration peaks per step (1.47 g and 0.79 g); steep running one peak (1.81 g), "no aerial phase, 40% faster stride frequency, and 40% shorter foot-ground contact time" than steep walking. https://europepmc.org/abstract/MED/32705391 (abstract read through Europe PMC)
- 9.4 to 39.2 degrees (Giovanelli et al.): one foot is always on the ground in both gaits; walking stride frequency about 28 % slower and stride length about 28 % longer than running; running duty factor averaged 62.7 %; both stride frequency and stride length fell at the steeper angles and slower belt speeds. https://europepmc.org/abstract/MED/26607247 and thesis PDF above.
- 0, 10, 20, 30 degrees at 0.6 m/s, 11 people (Robbins, Grimmitt, Hoogkamer, conference poster): ankle and toe-joint (MTP) dorsiflexion both increase with incline; at 30 degrees the two are negatively correlated (r2 = 0.79). No angle values in the text. https://cnrobbins.github.io/assets/uphillkinASB.pdf
- 6, 12, 20 degree ramps, self-paced, 19 men (Jiang et al. 2024, 10 mm heel drop): speed 2.34, 2.07, 1.74 m/s; stride length 140.07, 130.17, 117.87 cm; cadence 1.66, 1.59, 1.48 per second. https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/bioengineering-and-biotechnology/articles/10.3389/fbioe.2024.1385264/full
- 0 to +10 % grade (Leroux et al. 2002): more flexed hip, knee and ankle at foot contact, progressive forward tilt of pelvis and trunk, gradual increase in stride length. When only standing on the incline, trunk and pelvis stay aligned with true vertical. https://europepmc.org/abstract/MED/11809582
- 0, +/-3, +/-6, +/-9 degrees (Dewolf et al. 2018): "the trunk shows a forward tilt during uphill walking and a backward tilt during downhill walking"; stride period is largely unaffected by slope. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6842866

### 7. Hands-and-feet locomotion
- Adults crawling on hands and feet on a treadmill (MacLellan et al. 2012): speeds 0.5 to 3.0 km/h; 3 km/h "chosen to correspond approximately to a comfortable and natural speed for crawling"; also at 2 km/h on inclines of 13, 27 and 35 degrees. "Subjects tended to use a lateral footfall sequence... the footfall of a lower limb usually followed that of the ipsilateral upper limb." Overall no limb pairing (phase lag about 25 %); some people were more diagonal, some more lateral, and each kept their own pattern across speeds and inclines. Four, three or two limbs support at once. Incline did not change cycle duration. https://art.torvergata.it/retrieve/e291c0d4-b327-cddb-e053-3a05fe0aa144/MacLellan_Features%20of%20hand_2011.pdf (PDF)
- Young adults over ground (Cole et al. 2018): hands and knees 0.40 body lengths per second; hands and feet 0.46 body lengths per second at 4.20 steps per second. On hands and feet: pace-like (same-side limbs together) 56.1 % of strides, trot-like (diagonal limbs together) 36.6 %. On hands and knees: trot-like 93.1 %. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6345180
- Fastest 100 m on all fours: 14.55 s, Ryusei Yonee, Yonago, Japan, 24 September 2025. http://www.guinnessworldrecords.com/world-records/88771-fastest-100-m-running-on-all-fours

## Inferences
(Arithmetic or reasoning from the cited numbers. Not sourced statements.)

- Tobler in m/s: uphill 0.39 (20 deg), 0.19 (30 deg), 0.07 (40 deg); downhill 0.56, 0.26, 0.11. Tobler makes steep downhill only about 1.4 times faster than uphill. The GPS study (30 down equals 16 up) and race data say the real gap is larger.
- Fully record: 1000 m / 1641 s = 0.61 m/s vertical; 1920 m / 1641 s = 1.17 m/s (4.2 km/h) along the course. Average angle is 27.5 degrees if 52 % is rise over run, or 31.4 degrees if 1,920 m is the along-slope length (asin(1000/1920)). The sources do not say which. 60 % = 31 degrees.
- Manitou Incline record: 613 m / 1036 s = 0.59 m/s vertical; about 1,547 m along the slope gives 1.49 m/s. Women: 0.51 m/s vertical. These are on steps.
- So the ceiling for elite athletes on 24 to 31 degree ground is about 0.6 m/s vertical and 1.2 to 1.5 m/s along the slope. The lab's sustainable 0.35 m/s vertical (0.70 m/s along a 30 degree belt) is a fit runner's steady effort. Naismith's 600 m per hour is 0.17 m/s vertical for an ordinary walker.
- GPS study, 30 degrees uphill: slow walk 1609 m / 5820 s = 0.28 m/s (flat 0.81 m/s); fast run 2.06 m/s.
- Mount Marathon: 2 mph = 0.89 m/s up, 12 mph = 5.4 m/s down. The slope angle was not in the fetched page.
- Slip angle for a standing person, theta = atan(mu): rubber on dry concrete 31 to 40 degrees; wet concrete 24 to 37; dry asphalt 42 (static), 27 to 39 (kinetic); wet asphalt 14 to 37; tire on grass (0.35) 19; climbing rubber on granite 44 to 50. Walking needs more friction than standing: on a 20 degree ramp the required value was 0.45 to 0.60 going down and 0.6 to 1.10 going up, against tan(20) = 0.36.
- Because ordinary shoe grip runs out at roughly 30 to 40 degrees and one person's ankle bend ran out at 24 degrees, a believable character should stop flat-footed straight-up walking somewhere in the high 20s to mid 30s and switch to traversing, forefoot steps with hands, or all fours. This band is a reasoning estimate, not a sourced threshold.
- Jiang's "cadence" times stride length reproduces the speed (1.48 x 1.18 m = 1.74 m/s), so it is strides per second. From 6 to 20 degrees, self-chosen speed fell 26 %, stride length 16 %, stride rate 11 %. These speeds are high for 20 degrees and come from a short ramp, so use the ratios, not the absolute speeds.
- All fours: 100 m / 14.55 s = 6.9 m/s sprint record. Comfortable crawl 3 km/h = 0.83 m/s. 0.46 body lengths per second is about 0.8 m/s for a 1.75 m person (height assumed).
- Step-shape cue from the Army text: uphill on steep ground is pause-and-lock (rest step), toes out, then diagonal; downhill is bent knees, upright back, full sole, short steps.
