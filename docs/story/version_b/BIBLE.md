# LOST SIGNAL — story bible, Version B (Coronado)

Written 20 Nov 2026 as an alternative to Version A (*The Long Way Round*, `docs/story/STORY_BIBLE.md`).

It keeps every rule of Version A:
- a real open-world crime game first, that happens to teach English;
- the hero speaks every one of their own lines aloud, always within level;
- the Word Fog and Yasin's three translation tiers (word > sentence > syntax);
- optional phone lessons, never a stop for a lesson;
- drama and comedy, every scene for a reason;
- needs that make sense in the story;
- a hero that can be anyone.

It keeps the same syllabus order (`docs/story/CEFR_MAP.md`): mission 1 here teaches what mission 1 there teaches, and
so on to mission 31. Only the story, the cast, the places and the scenes are new.

Files:
- `BIBLE.md`: this file. The vision, the recording, the cast, the island, the structure, the systems, new locations.
- `SCRIPT.md`: every mission, beat by beat, in the Version A format.
- `MUSIC.md`: every music cue in words, with original lyrics.

## 1. The pitch
You are the cook's mate on a cargo ship anchored off Coronado. At two in the morning you step out on the stern deck to
record a voice message for your grandmother. Under your voice, your phone records something else: three men arguing
in English, a language you do not speak. Then a cry, and a splash. A man is in the black water.

You throw a life ring with a light. You jump in after him. You lose him in the dark. At dawn you crawl onto the beach
in front of the island's grand hotel, and you watch your ship sail away without you.

You are the only witness. You have the only recording. And you cannot understand a word of it.

**The tagline:** *The truth is on your phone. You just can't hear it yet.*

## 2. The theme, and why English is the story
**Version B's idea in one line: the language is the detective work.**

The mystery is a 76-second recording. At the start of the game it is noise. Each time the hero's English grows, the
player plays it again and understands more:
- first single words (a number, a day, a name);
- then sentences (who said what);
- then grammar (who *meant* what).

Every new level of English is a new clue. The player never learns English "for the test". They learn it because there
is a voice on their phone that says who killed Niko, and every new word brings them closer.

Three of the game's big twists are **grammar twists**. A structure the player meets in a mission changes the meaning
of a line they have heard for hours:
- **Present perfect (M14):** "She *has never seen* them." The captain is innocent.
- **First conditional (M18):** "*If* you call anyone, they'll kill you." This is not a threat. It is a warning. The man
  we hated is the one who tried to save Niko.
- **Relative clauses (M19):** "You know *who he works for*." There is someone above the killer.

The player does not just hear the twist. They *earn* it with a piece of grammar. That is the teaching, and it is the
thrill, at the same time.

The second theme is **being heard**. The hero is a witness nobody listens to, because the hero cannot speak. The
villain's whole plan depends on that silence. The story ends in a hearing room, where the hero plays the recording to
the room and explains every line in their own English. Voice is the verb, and in the last act, voice is the weapon.

The story question: *Will you take the plane home and forget the night, or stay and find the words?*

## 3. Pillars
1. **A game first.** Chases on foot and by car, a boat chase, stealth in the container yard, a food truck that crosses
   the whole island, stakeouts, a stolen-car ring, money, jobs. A player who already speaks perfect English should
   still want to solve this.
2. **The recording is the engine.** Every act ends with a *Listening* scene. The player always knows the next clue is
   in their pocket, waiting for their English.
3. **Every scene earns its place.** Each mission has a *Why it's here* line: story, play, language. If it does only one,
   it is cut or merged.
4. **Noir, with the lights on.** The story is dark (a murder, a frame, a corrupt badge). The people are warm and funny:
   Dette's food truck, Hex's terrible mixtape, Magda's ping-pong tyranny, Dez's cowardice, Taras and Kofi's endless
   bet. Every heavy scene has a light one before it.
5. **The whole island.** The story moves from the long beach in the south (where the hero washes up) to the naval base
   in the north (where the Coast Guard is), from the container yard to the hill, and back to the sea where it started.
6. **Anyone can be the hero.** Section 9.

## 4. The recording ("the Signal")
### 4.1 What it is, in the world
- The hero records a voice message to GRAN on their phone at 01:58 on the stern deck. They talk in their own language.
- They stop talking when the argument starts, but they do not stop the recording. The phone keeps recording in its
  waterproof pouch (Niko's gift) all through the splash, the throw of the life ring, and the jump.
- The file is 76 seconds long. It is saved as `voice_0158.m4a`. The game's evidence app shows it as a waveform with
  coloured layers.

### 4.2 Why nobody can just "listen to it" for the hero
This is the first question any player asks. The story answers it four ways:
1. **It is a bad recording.** Wind, waves, an outboard engine, a marine radio, and the hero's own voice on top of
   everything. Even a native speaker hears only bits. (Section 4.4: the audio layers.)
2. **The hero cannot explain it.** Without English, the hero cannot say *what* it is, *where*, *when*, or *who* is who.
   A police officer who gets a phone pushed at her by a wet, shaking stranger hears "a sailor talking to their family
   and some wind". (This happens in P3. It is the saddest moment of the prologue.)
3. **The ship's official story is against the hero.** The ship's log says that Second Officer Niko dela Cruz and the
   cook's mate "went ashore on the night launch and did not return". Two deserters. Missing seafarers are common. The
   police have no body and no report of a crime.
4. **The hero does not know whom to trust.** The voice that said "Who's there?" knows a witness exists. Until the hero
   knows whose voice it is, showing the recording to the wrong person could be the last thing they do. (In M6 the hero
   nearly plays it to Crosse. The phone is dead. The player understands how close that was only in M20.)

### 4.3 How it works in play
- **The Signal app** on the phone holds the recording. It can be played any time, anywhere. Each line is a subtitle
  under the waveform, in Word Fog.
- **The fog is per word and live.** It uses the same word list as all the NPC lines in the game. A word the player has
  met is sharp in the recording too. So a player who does lots of side content hears more, earlier.
- **Clues are per line and scripted.** A line "clicks" (a sharp sound, the line turns gold, a card goes up on the case
  wall) only when two things are true:
  1. the audio layer that hides it has been cleaned (section 4.4), and
  2. the mission that teaches its structure is done (the table in 4.5).
  This keeps the story in order, while the fog itself rewards every player for every word.
- **The translation tiers work on the recording, with one rule:** they cannot translate a line that has not clicked.
  A tap on a grey word in an unclicked line shows *"(too much noise)"*. This stops the tiers from solving the mystery,
  and it is true to life: you cannot translate what you cannot hear.
- **Tiredness and hunger make it harder.** At low energy or high hunger the recording's fog is thicker (the same rule
  as NPC speech). So the story's *Listening* scenes always start after a meal or a sleep. That is the needs system
  giving the clue scenes their calm.
- **The Case Wall.** A cork board in Magda's radio room at the Seafarers' Centre. Each clicked line pins a card with a
  photo or a drawing: a number, a boat, a face. It is the hub of the investigation and the map of who suspects whom.

the player drags sliders on a waveform until a voice is clear (it teaches nothing about English; it is the "detective
gadget" part of the puzzle).

### 4.7 The anonymous texts (the other "lost signal")
From M1, the hero gets short texts from an unknown number. They always arrive with one bar of signal and the number
never answers calls. They are in very simple English, so the player can read a little more of each:
- M1: `R U SAFE` (the player cannot read "R U" yet; Hex explains it in M3, to his great delight)
- M6: `DONT SHOW ANYONE`
- M13: `HOLT IS NOT THE ONE` (the player, at A2, reads it as a threat from Holt)
- M17: `I SAW THE LIGHT. THANK YOU`
- M20: `HE WILL COME FOR YOU NOW`
- M23: `LOOKOUT. 9PM. COME ALONE. STAR`
The texter is **Niko, alive**, hiding on the island with a cheap phone he turns on for one minute a day so it cannot be
traced. The word *Star* in M23 is the word he taught the hero on the ship. Only two people in the world know it.

## 5. Needs that make sense
The game keeps the three needs (`player/needs.gd`): **hunger, energy, bladder**. In Version B each one is tied to the
hero's job and to the investigation.

### Hunger: the cook who can't afford to eat
- The hero is a **cook**. Cooking is a real skill in the game (a short rhythm mini-game at a stove or a truck grill).
  Food you cook fills hunger more than food you buy, and food you cook *for others* builds trust (Magda, the bunkmates,
  Augie, Niko).
- **Crosse's $20 a day** (the "agency allowance") is the hunger clock of Act 1 and 2. It is also a leash. In M20 the
  hero stops taking his money, and hunger becomes a real cost again in Act 4 (on the run, eating at Dette's back door).
- Hungry: the fog on NPC speech and on the recording is thicker. Very hungry: shaking hands on the wheel and in fights.
- Ordering food grows with the level: "Can I have a coffee?" (M3) → arguing about a wrong order at the hotel (M11) →
  feeding fifty storm workers (M29).

### Energy: stakeouts and sleepless nights
- Sleep is the save, and it moves the clock. The hero sleeps in **bunk six** at the Seafarers' Centre (Act 1–3), on
  Augie's boat (Act 4), and in a borrowed room (Act 5).
- The investigation happens at night: the container yard (M7), the stakeout (M9), the warehouse (M15), the regatta
  party (M19), the quay (M20). Night work costs the next day: Dette's breakfast shift is at six.
- **Act 2's double life:** truck by day, Crosse's car jobs by night. Energy runs out, and the player must choose.
- Low energy: soft screen edges, the hero yawns over lines, thicker fog. Coffee from the Amber Ave cafés is a small
  boost and an ordering moment.
- Taras snores. Earplugs from the 24h store are a real item ($2.49) and a running joke.

### Bladder: stakeouts and doors
- **Stakeouts:** in a parked food truck (M5), on a small sailboat whose toilet pump is very loud (M9), in a warehouse
  where you must stand still (M15). A full bladder shortens how long you can hide.
- **Doors:** the food truck has no toilet. On the strip, the only place that lets you in is Glossy Kings car wash. Asking
  to use it (M5) is how the hero first sees the back bay, and the red car with the Oriel Star sticker on the window.
  The need opens the investigation.
- Comedy: Dette's rule, "No one leaves my truck in the rush. Not for God, not for nature."

### Rule for writers
A need never interrupts a cinematic. Before a big scene the game tops the needs up quietly, or the script gives the
hero a reason to eat, sleep or go first. Every *Listening* scene starts fed and rested.

**Why five acts.** The CEFR levels map onto the acts, so the hero's English and the hero's power grow together:
- **A1**: the hero can only react: things happen *to* them (the beach, the break-in, the chase);
- **A2**: the hero can follow and ask: they find the container, but they believe the wrong story;
- **B1**: the hero can understand *why*: the grammar twists clear two suspects and reveal the real one;
- **B1+**: the hero can tell their story: they need it, because now they are the suspect;
- **B2**: the hero can persuade and lead: they run the final night and speak in the hearing.

### The clock
The *Oriel Star* runs a six-week loop round the Pacific. On the day the hero washes up, the game shows a quiet line in
the phone's calendar: **ORIEL STAR — ETA CORONADO — 42 DAYS.** The counter is always there. It is the date of the
last shipment, and the date of the climax. It is a soft clock: missions move it, free roam does not.

## 9. Any player, any look
The same five rules as Version A, applied to this cast.

**Rule 1: The hero's own lines are never gendered.** "I am {NAME}. I am a cook."

**Rule 2: Nobody's attitude to the hero depends on the look.** Fixed address words, the same for everyone:
- Crosse: "my friend" (it gets colder every act);
- Dette: "sugar";
- Hex: "chief";
- Magda: "sailor";
- Augie: "Cook" (as a name, with a capital);
- Ferrer: "{SURNAME}" and later "{NAME}";
- Holt: "Galley";
- Niko: "*kusinero*" ("cook" in Tagalog; it is a joke between them from the first scene);
- Taras: "young person", even to a 45-year-old hero. Kofi: "my friend".

**Rule 3: Third-person lines use tokens** `{they}` `{them}` `{their}` `{they're}`, marked ⚥ in SCRIPT.md. There are few:
police radio, Crosse on the phone, the hearing. The hero is almost always spoken *to*.

**Rule 4: No romance gate.** Niko is a friend and a teacher. The bond is the night watch and the word *Star*.

**Rule 5: Danger is equal.** Holt's grip, Crosse's threats, the frame, the boat chase: the same for every look.

## 10. Systems the story uses
- **Mission markers.** One icon per main character. Main missions open by story and by time of day (many are at
  night), so the day planner (energy) is a real choice.
- **EYES (instead of a wanted level).** A meter of how much attention the hero draws: from the police (reckless
  driving, trespass) and from Crosse's people (asking questions in the wrong place). When it is high:
  - police: a stop is a conversation; the hero must talk their way out in English (name, address, what they are
    doing). Fail and they spend a night in the station (a soft cost: lost day, Ferrer annoyed);
  - Crosse's people: a grey van follows you; you must lose it (drive, hide, or blend into a crowd).
  - In Act 4 (the frame) Eyes never goes below one bar. The whole island is looking for you.
- **Driving** from M8 (Dette's truck, then cars). **Boats** from M9 (Augie's dinghy) and M24 (the chase).
- **Money.** The truck (steady), the car-wash mini-game (small), Crosse's car jobs (big, and a trap), Augie's dive
  jobs (Act 3+), interpreting jobs (post-game).
- **Cooking.** A rhythm mini-game at any stove or grill. Hunger refill, trust with allies, tips.
- **Stealth.** The yard, the warehouse, the yacht club. Hide, listen, read signs. Being seen raises Eyes.
- **The Signal app, the Case Wall** (section 4).
- **The phone:** Pocket (lessons), Notes (every phrase heard, with the voice clip), Map, Bank, Messages, Signal.
- **Voice notes home.** Once per act the hero records a message to Gran (S7). The player speaks it aloud. At first it
  is in the player's language; from Act 3 the game invites the hero to add one English sentence "so Gran can practise
  too". In the epilogue the whole message is in English. Gran's replies are recorded per localisation.
- **Mission failure:** retried from the last checkpoint. Only actions fail. Slow English never fails a mission.

## 13. Notes for implementation
- Line format and marks exactly as Version A (section 10 of `STORY_BIBLE.md`).
- `*` after a name = recorded in each localisation language (GRAN*, MOSS*, YOU* in the recording).
- Music cue ids are `MUS-B01` … (the B is for Version B, so they never clash with Version A's `MUS-xx`).
- The recording is one audio file per layer (L0–L5), mixed live by the Signal app, plus a per-line subtitle file with
  word-level timing for the fog.
- A line's click = a story flag (`signal_line_04_clicked`). The Case Wall reads the flags.
