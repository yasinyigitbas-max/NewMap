# WISH YOU WERE HERE: story bible
Written 20 Nov 2026. A new story, designed from the start for **absolute beginners: A1 only.**

Yasin read Versions A, B and C and found the mistake they all make: they climb to B2, and nobody gets from zero to
B2 in a 25-hour game. This story keeps all of Yasin's wishes:
- a real game first, like GTA;
- subtle teaching;
- drama and comedy;
- cinematic scenes, emotion cues and music in words;
- adventures from one end of the island to the other;
- needs that make sense;
- a story that works for any player character, whatever they look like.

But it is *built* for A1: the plot, the hero's job, the clues and the climax all work with A1 English.

Files in this folder:
- `BIBLE.md`: this file.
- `A1_MAP.md`: the A1 syllabus, mission by mission, plus the rules for every line the player says.
- `SCRIPT_1_PROLOGUE_TO_ACT2.md` and `SCRIPT_2_ACT3_TO_EPILOGUE.md`: every mission, beat by beat.
- `MUSIC.md`: every music cue, in words, with original lyrics.
- `NEW_LOCATIONS.md`: what to build.

## 1. The pitch
For a year, your brother or sister **ARI** has sent you postcards from an island called Coronado. Every postcard
has a photo on the front, and on the back, a few short sentences in English. Ari was learning it, and you couldn't
read them.

The last postcard has a bus ticket in it, and four words you can read, because they're a name and a number:
**"Come. 14 Lemon Street. — Ari"**

You arrive. Ari is not at the bus station. Ari is not at 14 Lemon Street. Ari's phone is off. In Ari's room you find
a key, a delivery-rider jacket with an orange **ZIPP** logo, and a box with every postcard you sent back.

To pay the rent and to look for Ari, you put on the jacket. You become a delivery rider: one of a hundred people
from forty countries on bikes and scooters, carrying burritos, flowers and phone chargers to every corner of the
island.

And slowly you understand: Ari's postcards were not just postcards. They were a map.

**One line:** *Find your sibling with the only English you have: small words, said at the right door.*

## 2. Why this story fits A1 (the design idea)
A1 English is small. At A1 a person can:
- say who they are;
- ask and answer simple questions;
- buy things;
- follow and give directions;
- describe a person and a place in short sentences;
- talk about their day;
- say what they can do;
- understand short messages and signs.

They can't argue, tell long stories, persuade or explain. So this story never asks the hero to. Instead:

1. **The hero's job is an A1 job.**
   - A delivery rider's English is A1 English: names, numbers, addresses, "Here you are", "Is this 22B?",
     "Turn left", "Have a nice day", "Sorry, I'm late".
   - Every delivery repeats the core language in a new place, with a new person and a new joke.
   - The job is a **recycling machine**, which is exactly what a beginner needs: the same structures a thousand
     times, never boring, because the island is different every time.
2. **The mystery is an A1 mystery.**
   - Ari's postcards are written in A1 English, because Ari was a beginner too. Each one describes a place: *"There
     is a big tree. There is a blue door. I can see the bridge."*
   - To solve the mystery, the player must *read A1 descriptions and match them to real places* on the island. The
     detective tools are A1 questions: *"Do you know this person? Where is…? Is there a…? What time…?"*
3. **The climax is an A1 climax.**
   - No courtroom speech and no persuasion. The last mission is a **delivery run**: twelve deliveries across the
     night island, each one a few short words at a door: *"This is yours."*
   - The final confrontation is won by many small voices, not one big one: a hundred riders, each saying one simple
     true sentence.
   - **Theme: small words, big voice.**
4. **The power curve is not the language.** The hero's English stays A1 (it grows inside A1: from single words to
   short sentences joined with *and, but, because*). The hero grows stronger through:
   - **the map:** the island opens district by district;
   - **people:** friends, customers, the rider network;
   - **vehicles:** feet → bike → e-bike → scooter → the hub van in the finale;
   - **the Rider Rating:** stars, explained in section 6.

This is honest. The game doesn't promise A1 in 25 hours; it *covers every A1 topic* and repeats it until it sticks.
The optional Pocket lessons and the post-game delivery work give the extra hours that real A1 needs. A2 can be the
sequel: a second island, or a second season.

## 3. Pillars
1. **A game first.**
   - A delivery rider in an open city is a great GTA job: speed, traffic, shortcuts, tips, ratings, rivals.
   - Add a missing sibling, a crooked company, night chases and a whole island to search.
   - Someone who speaks perfect English should want to find Ari.
2. **Small words.** Every line the player says is A1. Never above (rules in `A1_MAP.md`).
3. **Every scene earns its place.** Each mission has a *Why it's here*: story, play, language.
4. **Warm, funny, a little dangerous.** It's a comedy of doors (every customer is a sketch), with a real thriller
   underneath. The tone sits between a summer road movie and a crime story.
5. **The whole island.** Deliveries go everywhere. Every district has customers, a clue and a friend.
6. **Anyone can be the hero, anyone can be Ari.** Section 9.

## 4. How the teaching hides
### A. Voice is the verb (as before)
The player says every line of the hero aloud. Every line is A1. A missed voice check never blocks the game: after
three tries the NPC understands from the gesture ("Ah, this one? Okay!").

### B. Zippy: the app is the teacher (and the villain's voice)
The ZIPP delivery app talks to the rider all day, through an earbud: a cheerful synthetic voice called **ZIPPY**.
- Zippy speaks **perfect A1**: short, slow, clear sentences, because the app was built for riders from forty
  countries. "New order. One pizza. Go to 31 Ocean Boulevard. Turn left." "Great job! Five stars!"
- The player hears hundreds of perfect A1 model sentences without a single lesson, because it's the job.
- It is also funny. Zippy is relentlessly positive about everything, including rain, crashes and being bitten by a
  dog: "Oh no! A dog! You are doing great!"
- **The twist (Act 3):** the "Night Orders" also come through Zippy. The cheerful voice is how the boss gives
  criminal orders. In the finale the hero turns Zippy against him (section 7).

### C. The Word Fog and the translation tiers
As before:
- NPCs speak naturally; words the player has met are sharp, and the rest is soft grey;
- "Sorry?" and "Slower, please." always work;
- Yasin's tiers sit under the fog (word > sentence > syntax).

**At A1 the fog matters more**, so the game is generous:
- **faces and hands:** NPCs point, mime and hold things up;
- **the job context:** at a door, the customer is always saying one of about twenty things;
- **the postcards:** written exactly at the player's level, so they are always readable when the story needs them.

### D. The postcards (the A1 reading system)
Ari's 24 postcards are the story's spine and the player's reading practice:
- Each one is 3–6 A1 sentences about a place, with a photo or drawing on the front.
- When the hero gets one (some are in Ari's box, some are hidden at places, some are mailed late), it goes into the
  **Postcards** app.
- Unknown words are grey (the fog), the translation tiers work on them, and they are **always solvable**: the
  sentences use only what the player has met by then, plus one new word with a picture.
- Reading a postcard and finding its place is the main detective loop.

### E. Pocket (optional lessons, as before)
Every structure the player meets has a short lesson with practice. The game offers it when the player has met the
structure three times.

### F. The Rider Bench (A1 small talk)
Between orders, riders wait on a bench outside **Tía Lupe's taco window** on the strip. Sitting there brings short,
repeatable A1 conversations with the rider friends: *How are you? Where are you from? Do you like…? What time is
it? Is it busy today?* It's the game's "social hub", and its free speaking practice: small talk at a bench, the
most real A1 there is.

## 5. Needs: the rider's body
A delivery rider's life *is* the needs system. That's why it fits so well.
- **Hunger:** you carry food all day that you can't afford.
  - The smell of every burger is a joke and a pain: the hero's stomach growls at doors, and customers notice
    ("...Are you hungry?").
  - Tía Lupe gives riders the day's last tacos at 10 p.m. Some customers tip with food.
  - Eating is cheap, kind and social. Hunger is the comedy of poverty, never cruel.
- **Energy:** shifts are long, and night orders pay double.
  - The game's money pressure is rent; energy is the price.
  - Sleep is the save. At low energy, riding gets wobbly, and Zippy says "You look tired! You are doing great!"
- **Bladder: the Toilet Map** (a real rider problem, made a system).
  - Most businesses say "Restrooms are for customers only."
  - Every business on the island has a hidden "toilet attitude". The hero learns it by asking: **"Excuse me, can I
    use the toilet, please?"** (A1 *can* for permission).
  - Some say yes (and are marked on the phone's map with a little toilet icon); some say no; one says yes only if
    you say please twice; one says yes and then gives you a lecture about his toilet.
  - The Toilet Map is a meta-map of the island's kindness. Riders trade toilet tips on the Rider Bench. It is a
    running joke with a real heart: the kind places are where the friends are.
- **Rule:** needs never interrupt a cinematic or a climax. The game tops them up before.

## 6. Systems (the GTA layer)
- **Deliveries:** the main income. An order is a pick-up (a restaurant or shop) and a drop-off (a door). They come
  in three kinds:
  - **Day orders:** normal; A1 exchanges at doors.
  - **Rush orders:** timed; a chase against the clock through traffic.
  - **Night Orders** (Act 3): pay triple, a sealed orange bag, "don't open, don't talk". They are the crime.
- **The Rider Rating: stars** (GTA's wanted stars, turned upside down).
  - Five stars for good deliveries; they fall for late, wrong or rude ones.
  - High stars unlock better orders, richer districts and, in the story, **trust**: the rich south end only orders
    from 4-star riders, and the base gate only lets in 5-star riders.
  - **Heat:** a second meter in Act 3, separate from the stars. How much the boss's people are looking for you.
- **Vehicles:** walking (prologue) → Ari's old bike (Act 1) → a rented Zipp e-bike (Act 2) → Minh's scooter (Act 3)
  → the hub's orange van (the finale). Cars exist in the world. The hero doesn't drive one until the end: a beginner
  isn't a driver, and a beginner's world is a bike's world. It's true, and it makes the island feel big.
- **Wrong door:** delivering to the wrong address is a comedy event, not a fail. A different person, a different
  sketch, and you try again. The doors are the funniest content in the game; there should be many.

## 7. The plot in one page
**Prologue: The Postcards.** At home: a year of postcards from Ari, unreadable. The last one: a ticket and an
address. The bus over the Bridge. Ari isn't at the bus station.

**Act 1: Lemon Street (A1, the first steps).** The hero finds 14 Lemon Street on the hill: a yellow house divided
into rooms.
- Ari's room has a key, the ZIPP jacket and the box of postcards. The landlady, **MRS. OKORO**, says Ari left
  three weeks ago and the rent is late.
- The hero becomes a Zipp rider in Ari's jacket (the hub is at the docks; the hub manager, **RAY**, is warm, fun and
  "everybody's friend").
- Deliveries teach the island; the Rider Bench gives friends: **MINH**, **TOMÁS** and **NOOR**.
- The hero starts reading Ari's postcards and finds the first place: a bakery. The baker, **SOFIA**, knows Ari: "Ari came
  every morning. Then… no."
- The act ends with a postcard that wasn't in the box. It is pushed under the door, in Ari's handwriting, dated
  *last week*. **Ari is alive, and hiding.**

**Act 2: The Route (A1, describing people and days).** The hero rebuilds Ari's daily life by asking the island:
*"Do you know this person? Does she / he come here? What time?"*
- Each postcard leads to a place, a person and a piece of Ari's routine: the library, the laundromat, the pier, the
  hotel, the base gate.
- The hero finds out that Ari took **Night Orders** for Zipp, and that something happened on Ari's last night
  order.
- Ray is kind, and gives the hero Ari's old phone charger, "from the lost box".
- The act ends with the hero at a place from a postcard, *the old boathouse at the marina*, where Ari's bike is
  hidden. In its basket: a hub key card and a note: "**Don't trust Zippy.**"

**Act 3: Night Orders (A1, now and before).** The hero takes Night Orders to find out what Ari found.
- The orange bags contain **passports**: the riders' own passports. Ray "keeps them safe" for the riders at the
  hub, and the company sells their identities on the side.
- Ari found out, took the box of passports from the hub's safe, hid it, and has been hiding from Ray's people ever
  since.
- Ari's postcards are a trail to the box, written so that only the hero would follow it.
- Ray, still smiling, starts to suspect the new rider in Ari's jacket. The Heat meter rises; there's a chase
  through the night island.
- The hero finds Ari at last, in the eye of the act's storm: on a small sailing boat owned by an old sailor,
  **CAPTAIN BEA**, who has been hiding Ari.
- The reunion is the emotional centre of the game, and it is in A1, which makes it stronger: *"Ari. It's me. I'm
  here."*

**Act 4: Last Delivery (A1, everything).** The box is in the last place on the postcards. Ray's people know too.
- The riders' passports must go back to the riders, all over the island, in one night, before Ray takes them back
  and the company's lawyers get to the police first.
- The finale is a delivery run: twelve doors, twelve "This is yours." The whole rider network rides with the hero.
  - Minh blocks a road with forty e-bikes.
  - Tomás rides the van.
  - Noor hacks Zippy, so the cheerful app voice tells *all* riders on the island the truth, in perfect A1:
    "Attention, riders. Your passport is not at the hub. Go to the pier."
- **The confrontation at the pier.** Ray, with lawyers and a smile. A hundred riders arrive in the dark, headlights
  on. One by one, each says one A1 sentence to the police and the cameras:
  - "My name is Minh. This is my passport. It was at the hub."
  - "My name is Tomás. I am from Brazil. I work for Zipp. Ray has my passport."
  - The hero's sentence is the last: "My name is {NAME}. This is Ari. We are not afraid."
- **The epilogue.** The hero writes a postcard home: the player writes it, in their own A1 English, the final free
  production of the game. On the front, a photo the player chose from their own playthrough. On the back, what they
  write. It ends with the fixed phrase: ***Wish you were here.***

## 9. Any hero, any Ari
- **The hero's lines are never gendered.** "I am {NAME}. I am a rider."
- **Ari's gender is the player's choice**, separate from the hero's: brother, sister, or sibling. Ari's own lines
  are never gendered. NPCs' lines *about* Ari use tokens (`{ari_they}`, `{ari_them}`, `{ari_their}`) and are marked
  ⚥A in the script; actors record one take per pronoun. Because the whole game is "Do you know this person?",
  there are more such lines than in other versions, so every one is short and A1.
- **Bonus:** the hero practises *he / she / they* and *his / her / their* every time they describe Ari, the most
  natural way possible to practise A1 pronouns and possessives. The player chooses which they practise.
- **Nicknames are neutral:** Minh says "baby" to everyone, Tomás says "amigo", Mrs. Okoro says "my child", and Ray
  says "champ".
- **No romance gates. Equal danger.**

## 10. The Doors (recurring customers: the comedy of the island)
Each regular orders several times across the game. Each visit is a 30-second sketch with an A1 exchange, and each
has a tiny story that finishes by the end.

|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Mr. Pemberton** | The hill | Orders one banana. Every day. 7 a.m. | Never says why | Act 4: he's been leaving the banana peel on the doorstep as a signal to his neighbour across the street, whom he's too shy to talk to. The hero delivers his first note to her: "Hello. I am Arthur. Do you like bananas?" |
| **The Delgado twins** | The apartments | Two 8-year-olds who order pizza with their mother's phone | Every time, a new excuse | The mother answers the door in Act 3 and tips double "for being nice to my monsters" |

## 12. Structure
| Part | A1 stage | Missions | Hours (est.) | The hero can… |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Prologue: *The Postcards* | Starter | P1–P2 | 0.5 | say their name, numbers, hello |
| Act 1: *Lemon Street* | A1 part 1: me and my things | 1–6 | 6 | introduce themselves, ask for things, follow directions, read an address |
| Act 2: *The Route* | A1 part 2: people and days | 7–12 | 7 | describe a person, talk about routines and times, ask about someone |
| Act 3: *Night Orders* | A1 part 3: now, ability and the past of "be" | 13–18 | 7 | say what is happening now, what they can do, where things were |
| Act 4: *Last Delivery* | A1 review: everything, faster | 19–22 | 4 | use all of it under pressure, in short, true sentences |
| Epilogue: *Wish You Were Here* | Free writing | E | 0.5 | write a postcard |

After the story: the island stays open. Deliveries go on forever (each with a random A1 exchange, the best review
tool in the game), plus the side strands. A2 is the sequel's job.
